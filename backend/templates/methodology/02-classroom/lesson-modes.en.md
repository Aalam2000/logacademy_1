# Lesson Modes: how a child knows at any moment what is allowed and what is not

### Practical classroom management scheme — what to announce to the class and when

---

## Why is this needed

A child's working memory is limited, and everything that is wasted takes up space for understanding the material (more details in the material [“Pedagogy for those who teach 'simply about the complex'”](/dashboard/methodology/01-pedagogy/pedagogy-basics), section on cognitive load). There is a load that is usually not talked about separately — **social uncertainty**. If a child has to constantly decide during the lesson "is it okay to speak now?", "can I interrupt?", "is it okay if I approach my neighbor?", part of their attention is not on the code but on guessing the rules of the game. The less they spend on this, the more remains for the subject.

The solution is not "discipline" in the sense of prohibitions, but a small set of **four named modes** plus one emergency signal that works over any of them. The mode is announced aloud, it has a clear name, and the rules within it are the same every time. The child does not memorize the rules anew in each lesson — they learn the system once and then simply understand which mode the class is currently in.

This is the same principle as Jacob Kounin's: good classroom management is a structure that prevents violations from occurring, rather than a reaction to a violation that has already happened.

---

## Mode 1 — “Listening” (explaining new material)

**What is allowed:** sit quietly, look at the teacher or the screen, raise your hand if something is unclear, and wait until you are addressed.

**What is not allowed:** speak without permission, interrupt, get up from your seat, talk to a neighbor.

**How to announce:** briefly and always with the same words, for example: *“Mode ‘listening’. Hands — if a question”*. For the first few times, say this completely, then a gesture or just the word “listening” will suffice.

**Why this way:** this is the most “expensive” moment of the lesson in terms of load — the teacher is providing new information, and any extraneous noise (comments, movement, whispering) competes for the same narrow bottleneck of working memory. Here, the strictness of the rules is not a whim, but a protection of the most valuable resource of the lesson.

**Important:** the “listening” mode should not last longer than 7–10 minutes in a row — otherwise, even with perfect discipline, attention physically drops without any violation of the rules.

---

## Mode 2 — "Thinking Together" (discussion, warm-up, question to the class)

**What is allowed:** raise your hand and briefly answer when asked personally; have a short and quiet discussion with a neighbor before answering ("whispering, 10 seconds").

**What is not allowed:** shout out an answer without being called; turn the discussion into a separate conversation unrelated to the topic.

**How to announce:** *"Mode 'thinking together' — raise your hand"*. This mode works well with the Peer Instruction method: everyone answers individually → discusses the answer with a neighbor → answers again.

**Why this way:** the mode is intentionally livelier than "listening" — it trains the type of thinking for which warm-up questions and quizzes exist. But it remains controlled by one rule: the person called speaks, not the one who is louder.

---

## Mode 3 — "Working Independently" (practice on the computer, independent assignment)

**What is allowed:** work at your own pace, quietly ask a neighbor about the assignment, raise your hand and wait for the teacher to come over.

**What is not allowed:** call the teacher loudly across the class, get up and go to the teacher yourself (except in emergencies), engage in unrelated activities until you raise your hand "I am finished".

**How to announce:** *"Mode 'working independently'. Need help — hand up, I will come over"*.

**Why this way:** this is the only mode where the burden on the child is greater than on the teacher. The teacher's task here is not to speak, but to walk around the class and provide targeted help to each student in their zone of proximal development. The rule "hand up and wait" protects this: if calling out across the room is allowed, the teacher will respond to the loudest student, not to the one who truly needs help right now.

## Mode 4 — "Playing / Working in a Team" (activities like "Living Computer")

**What is allowed:** to move, talk within your team, be louder than usual — this is the only mode where this is consciously permitted.

**What is not allowed:** to disturb the other team with your voice; to continue the activity after the general stop signal.

**How to announce:** *“We are starting the game. The stop signal works here too”* — and briefly remind that at the signal (see below) everyone freezes, even in the middle of a sentence.

**Why this way:** this is the noisiest and most discipline-risky mode, so it must have a reliably trained exit, not just an entrance. Otherwise, the teacher may find themselves in a situation where twenty children in a role play do not respond to their voice — this is what is called "losing the class."

---

## Reset Signal — Emergency Stop Over All Modes

One short, always the same signal that interrupts **any** of the four modes and returns the class to "listening." For example: *“If you hear my voice — clap once”* and 3 seconds of silence; or the teacher's raised hand, to which the class silently responds with the same gesture.

The signal must always be the same, without variations like "today a clap, tomorrow a whistle": any variability destroys the automatic reaction for which the signal exists.

---

## How to Introduce the System in the First 10 Minutes of the First Lesson

The system cannot just be announced — it needs to be **trained on neutral material** before relying on it during the actual explanation:

1. Announce: “In our lessons, we will have four modes, so everyone understands what is allowed and what is not” — and briefly name all four, one sentence for each.
2. Immediately practice the reset signal on something trivial: “Now I will say something, and you respond to the signal” — twice in a row, until the reaction becomes quick and united.
3. Try to enter each mode in turn and exit it on a neutral topic (“what is your favorite color?” instead of actual material) — no more than 30 seconds per mode.
4. Only after this, move on to the lesson content — already in the "listening" mode.

These 10 minutes pay off from the very first transition between blocks of the lesson: there is no need to explain the rules in the middle of explaining the material.

## Cheat Sheet

| Mode | Allowed | Not Allowed | Entry Phrase |
| :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: |
| Listening | Watch, raise hand with a question | Speak without permission, stand up | “Listening mode” |
| Thinking Together | Raise hand, give a short answer, whisper to a neighbor for 10 seconds | Shout out | “Thinking Together - hands” |
| Working Independently | Own pace, hand up for help, quietly ask a neighbor | Call across the class, stand up on your own | “Working Independently - hand, I will come” |
| Playing / in a Team | Move, be louder, speak in a team | Disturb another team, continue after the signal | “We start the game, the stop signal works” |
| Reset Signal | — | — | “If you hear a voice - clap” + pause 3 seconds |

---

## The Main Rule of the System

Modes work only under one condition — **the teacher applies them consistently every time, without exceptions**. If today shouting in “listening” mode was overlooked, and tomorrow a remark is made for it, children learn not the rule, but that the rule is optional. This is not strictness for the sake of strictness: consistent rules make the lesson predictable for the child — and thus free their attention for the subject itself.
